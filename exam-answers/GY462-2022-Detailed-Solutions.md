# QUESTION 1 — Real Estate Investment: Risks, Opportunities & Fund Structures

## Q1a — Main risks that real estate investors need to take into account [5 pts]

*(Lecture 1)*

### Standard Answer

Real estate carries five principal downside risks:

1. **Vacancy risk** — the property is unlet, generating zero income while fixed costs (maintenance, insurance) continue. This is typically the most impactful operational risk.

2. **Tenant default risk** — an existing tenant fails to pay rent or goes insolvent mid-lease. Unlike a bond default, the landlord must also re-let the space.

3. **Liquidity risk** — real estate cannot be sold quickly. Transactions take months; bid-ask spreads widen in downturns; the investor may be forced to sell at a discount.

4. **Market / repricing risk** — cap rates expand (yields rise) as interest rates increase or sentiment deteriorates, compressing asset values even if NOI is stable.

5. **Operating cost / capital expenditure risk** — unexpected repair costs, refurbishment needs, or regulatory compliance (e.g. energy efficiency upgrades) erode net income.

|---------|---------|-------|
| 空置风险 Vacancy Risk | 无租金收入但成本持续 | 最常见运营风险 |

## Q1b — How do these risks feed into cash flow calculations and how do you assess their impact? [5 pts]

*(Lecture 1)*

### Standard Answer

Risks feed into financial analysis through two main channels:

**1. The discount rate** — higher risk demands a higher required rate of return (r = risk-free rate + risk premium). Vacancy risk, illiquidity, and tenant covenant quality are all reflected in a wider risk premium, raising the denominator and reducing present values.

**2. Scenario / sensitivity analysis ("flexing" the model)** — analysts stress-test the DCF model by adjusting key assumptions:
- Vacancy rate (e.g. 0%, 10%, 20% void periods)
- Exit yield / cap rate (e.g. ±50bps)
- Rental growth / rental concessions

By comparing the IRR or NPV under base, optimistic, and pessimistic scenarios, investors understand how sensitive their returns are to each risk factor. The variable that causes the largest swing in value is the one that deserves most attention in due diligence.

---

### 中文详解

**两条传导路径（必须都答到）：**

- **折现率 Discount Rate**：风险越高 → 折现率越高 → 分母越大 → 现值越低。空置风险、流动性风险、租户信用风险都直接推高折现率。

- **现金流敏感性分析（Flexing the model）**：把关键假设拉到悲观/乐观状态，看 IRR 或 NPV 的变化幅度。最敏感的变量（如空置率、退出 Cap Rate）应在尽调时重点审查。

**关键词**：Risk Premium 风险溢价 / Sensitivity Analysis 敏感性分析 / Vacancy Rate 空置率 / Exit Yield 退出资本化率

## Q1c — Main opportunities that arise from real estate investments [5 pts]

*(Lecture 1)*

### Standard Answer

Real estate offers three primary upside opportunities:

1. **Terminal value / capital appreciation** — the exit price at the end of the holding period is typically the dominant source of return (especially over short-to-medium hold periods). Buying below intrinsic value and selling when cap rates compress can generate significant gains. Value = NOI / Cap Rate: even a 50bps compression in cap rate at exit materially increases sale proceeds.

2. **Rental income and rental growth** — long-term leases provide stable, bond-like cash flows. When economic conditions are favourable, rent reviews allow rents to reset to higher market levels, compounding income over time.

3. **Operating cost savings** — managing properties efficiently (e.g. FRI leases that transfer cost risk to tenants, energy efficiency investments) directly increases NOI and thus asset value.

---

### 中文详解

**三大机会，要理解"为什么"：**

| 机会 | 来源 | 如何最大化 |
|-----|-----|---------|
| 终值/资本增值 Terminal Value | 退出时售价 > 买入价 | 低买高卖 + 在 Cap Rate 压缩时退出 |
| 租金收入与增长 Rental Income | 长期租约稳定现金流 | 向上租金调整条款 + 选强需求市场 |
| 运营成本节约 OpEx Savings | NOI 提升 → 价值提升 | FRI 租约转移成本 + 节能投资 |

**关键公式（必须记住）**：Value = NOI / Cap Rate → Cap Rate 越小，价值越大。所以"时机选择"非常重要。

## Q1d — Rents and sale value: why intrinsically linked? Why should a 'smart' asset manager focus on maximising rental values? [10 pts]

*(Lecture 1)*

### Standard Answer

**Why rents and sale value are intrinsically linked:**

Real estate is valued using the capitalisation (cap rate) method derived from the income approach:

$$\text{Sale Value} = \frac{\text{NOI}}{\text{Cap Rate}}$$

Since NOI is primarily driven by rental income (after deducting operating expenses), any increase in rent directly and proportionally increases the numerator. Because the cap rate is determined by the market (not the individual landlord), the only lever an asset manager controls is the numerator — rental income. A £1 increase in annual NOI translates into £1/Cap Rate in additional asset value. For example, at a 5% cap rate, an extra £100,000 of rent adds £2,000,000 in value.

**Why a smart asset manager should focus on maximising rents, not engineering the cap rate:**

An asset manager who seeks to inflate sale value by forcing a lower exit cap rate (e.g. by selling in a frothy market or structuring a leaseback with an inflated headline rent) is **window-dressing**. This is unsustainable and potentially illegal. By contrast, genuinely improving the asset's rental value — through leasing vacant space, extending leases, upgrading the building to attract better tenants — creates durable, verifiable income. A buyer will pay more because the income stream is real and recurring. Moreover, maximising rent protects value across all cap rate environments: even if cap rates widen and the market falls, a higher-income asset falls less in value and recovers faster.

---

**核心逻辑（必须能推导）：**

Value = NOI / Cap Rate

- Cap Rate 由市场决定，资产管理人**无法控制**
- NOI ≈ 租金 − 运营成本，资产管理人**可以控制**
- 所以：提高租金 = 提高 NOI = 直接提高资产价值

## Q1e — Lessons from Croydon and Leeds case studies: strategic asset management, revenue maximisation and risk mitigation [15 pts]

*(Lecture 1–3, Case Studies)*

### Standard Answer

Working on the two case studies — a suburban office building in Croydon and a mixed-use retail/office asset in Leeds — produced three key lessons across strategic asset management, revenue maximisation, and risk mitigation:

**1. Strategic Asset Management: align asset strategy with market fundamentals**

The Croydon asset demonstrated the importance of understanding location dynamics. Croydon is undergoing regeneration driven by the Westfield development and transport infrastructure improvements; however, vacancy rates in the local office market remain elevated. The key lesson is that the correct strategic posture — hold-and-refurbish versus sell now versus reposition for residential conversion — cannot be determined without first mapping the local supply/demand balance. A blanket "increase rents" strategy that ignores a structurally oversupplied market destroys occupancy.

The Leeds asset, operating in a more liquid and diversified northern city, showed that multi-tenant diversification reduces revenue concentration risk. No single tenant represented more than 20–25% of total income, limiting the damage of any one default.

**2. Revenue Maximisation: active leasing and rent review management**

Both assets highlighted that idle lease expiries are a principal source of value destruction. A proactive lease-regear strategy (extending leases before expiry, offering modest incentives to secure long-term covenants) stabilises income and reduces void periods. In Leeds, the analysis showed that a 10% vacancy assumption versus a 5% vacancy assumption changed the asset's IRR by approximately 150 basis points — a powerful illustration of how vacancy management directly drives returns.

Additionally, both assets demonstrated the importance of reviewing service charge provisions. In Croydon, service charges were partially unrecoverable (gross lease segments), inflating net operating costs. Re-structuring leases toward FRI terms at renewal would improve net income without changing the headline rent.

**3. Risk Mitigation: sensitivity analysis and exit strategy**

Modelling multiple scenarios (base/optimistic/pessimistic) on exit yield and vacancy revealed that the Croydon asset had asymmetric downside risk: any yield expansion beyond 25bps significantly reduced the sale value. This pointed toward an earlier exit while market cap rates remained compressed, rather than holding for additional rental growth in an uncertain lettings environment.

In Leeds, the lesson was different: because the lease structure provided income visibility for another seven years, holding through the cycle and targeting a sale when the income was most attractive to institutional buyers was the value-maximising strategy.

## Q1f — How does climate change alter perceptions of risks and opportunities? What are 'green' investors doing? [20 pts]

*(Lecture 10 — ESG)*

### Standard Answer

**How climate change alters the risk landscape:**

**Physical risks** — assets in flood-prone zones, coastal areas, or extreme-heat regions face higher insurance premiums, repair costs, and ultimately may become uninsurable. Buildings that do not meet future energy standards face **stranded asset risk**: as regulatory energy efficiency requirements tighten (e.g. UK EPC minimum requirements moving from E to B), non-compliant assets lose lettability and liquidity.

**Transition risks** — investors and occupiers are under increasing pressure from regulators, lenders, and beneficiaries to decarbonise portfolios. Assets with high embodied and operational carbon face tighter financing conditions and a growing **green discount** on value relative to certified green buildings.

**How climate change creates new opportunities:**

1. **Green premium** — buildings with strong ESG credentials (BREEAM 'Excellent', LEED Platinum, EPC A) command higher rents from corporate tenants with their own net-zero commitments. The rental premium for best-in-class green buildings in London has widened to 10–20% over comparable non-certified stock.

2. **Retrofit and value-add** — buying older, energy-inefficient assets at a discount and upgrading them to green standards (through insulation, LED systems, heat pumps, solar panels) creates a buy-renovate-exit strategy with attractive risk-adjusted returns, as the green premium is realised at sale.

3. **Sectoral tailwinds** — industrial/logistics assets required by the green economy (EV charging hubs, cold-chain logistics for sustainable food supply) offer strong long-term demand fundamentals.

**What green real estate investors are doing:**

- **Portfolio decarbonisation targets** — committing to net-zero operational carbon by 2050 (aligned with Paris Agreement), with interim targets for 2030.
- **Green leases** — incorporating provisions that require landlord and tenant to share energy data, jointly pursue energy efficiency, and commit to minimum EPC standards at lease renewal.
- **ESG due diligence** — integrating climate risk assessments (physical and transition) into the investment decision process, using tools like MSCI Climate VaR.
- **Engaging lenders** — accessing green finance (green bonds, sustainability-linked loans with pricing tied to ESG KPIs) to lower the cost of capital for certified assets.

---

**两大维度：气候变化改变了风险，也创造了机会**

**风险维度（新增风险）：**

## Q1g — Key features of real estate fund structures: management roles, incentives, waterfall [20 pts]

*(Lecture 5)*

### Standard Answer

Real estate private equity funds are structured as **Limited Partnerships (LPs)**, with two types of participants:

**Roles and management responsibilities:**

| | LP (Limited Partners) | GP (General Partner / Sponsor) |
|--|----------------------|-------------------------------|
| Role | Passive capital providers | Active investment decision-makers |
| Typical capital contribution | ~90% of total equity | ~10% of total equity |
| Management control | Minimal (to avoid becoming GP and incurring unlimited liability) | Full operational control |
| Typical investors | Pension funds, endowments, family offices | Blackstone, Cerberus, specialist RE managers |

The GP co-invests (typically 10% of equity) to create **alignment of interests**: if the GP has no "skin in the game," they could take excessive risks with LP capital.

**Incentives — Preferred Return and Promote:**

- **Preferred Return ("Pref")**: the LP must first receive a minimum annualised return (e.g. 8–9%) before the GP participates in any profit above that hurdle. This protects LPs and ensures the GP only earns a profit share after delivering meaningful value.

- **Promote / Carried Interest**: once the Pref is met, the GP receives a disproportionately large share of the residual profits (typically 20% of profits above Pref, while contributing only 10% of capital). This is the GP's primary performance incentive — it rewards outperformance significantly.

**Waterfall distribution:**

The waterfall defines the sequence in which cash is distributed:

1. **Return of LP capital** — LPs receive their invested capital back first.
2. **Return of GP capital** — GPs recover their co-investment.
3. **Preferred Return** — LPs receive the agreed hurdle rate on their capital (e.g. 9% p.a.).
4. **GP Catch-up** (sometimes) — GP receives a catch-up allocation until they reach their proportionate share.
5. **Residual split** — remaining profits split (e.g. 80% LP / 20% GP, with the 20% being the Promote).

The key insight: the GP invests 10% of capital but receives 20% of profits above the Pref. This **leverage on the promote** is why GPs are so motivated to maximise returns.

**Deal-by-deal vs. whole-fund calculation:**
- *Deal-by-deal* (common in the US): Promote calculated per transaction — GP can receive Promote on early winners even if later deals underperform. More favourable to GP.
- *Whole-fund* (common in the UK): Promote only calculated once all investments are aggregated. More protective for LPs.

---

### 中文详解

**三大核心（考试只需写这三块）：**

1. **LP/GP 结构**：LP 出钱不管事（有限责任），GP 管事并出少量资本（~10%）以对齐利益。

2. **激励机制**：
   - Pref（优先回报率）：LP 先拿到"保底"（如 9% 年化）
   - Promote（利润分成）：超过 Pref 后，GP 拿 20% 超额利润，尽管只出了 10% 资本 → 这就是 GP 努力工作的核心激励

3. **瀑布分配 Waterfall**：
   - ① 还 LP 本金 → ② 还 GP 本金 → ③ 给 LP 的 Pref → ④ 超额按 80/20 分
   - 顺序就像瀑布，水流方向不能逆

**关键词**：Limited Partnership 有限合伙制 / Preferred Return 优先回报率 / Promote / Carried Interest 附带权益 / Waterfall 瀑布分配 / Alignment of Interests 利益对齐 / Deal-by-Deal vs. Whole Fund

## Q1h — Different fund investors' priorities: institutional features, culture, country of origin [20 pts]

*(Lecture 5 — External Speaker: Damian Perry)*

### Standard Answer

Different institutional investors bring distinct mandates, constraints, and cultural expectations to real estate fund investment. Three key dimensions differentiate their behaviour:

**1. Investment mandate and liability profile:**

- **Pension funds** (e.g. UK local authority pensions, large Canadian plans like CPP) have long-dated, inflation-linked liabilities. They prioritise stable, income-generating assets with long leases (ideally RPI-linked) over capital appreciation. They have a strong preference for core and core-plus strategies, and are sensitive to liquidity provisions (ability to exit the fund).

- **University endowments** (e.g. Harvard, Yale) operate under an intergenerational equity mandate — they must preserve real capital value for future generations while distributing ~5% annually. They are more tolerant of illiquidity and often invest in value-add and opportunistic strategies in exchange for higher expected returns.

- **Sovereign Wealth Funds** (e.g. GIC Singapore, ADIA Abu Dhabi) have very long time horizons and often invest directly (bypassing the fund structure entirely). When they do use funds, they negotiate heavily on fee structures and prefer lower Promote thresholds.

**2. Cultural and governance expectations:**

- **Anglo-Saxon institutions** (UK, US, Canada, Australia) are comfortable with complex fund structures (waterfall mechanics, leverage, carried interest) and focus heavily on IRR benchmarks and relative performance against peers.

- **Continental European investors** (Germany, Netherlands, Nordics) tend to be more risk-averse, with a preference for lower leverage, longer holding periods, and sustainability criteria (ESG compliance is often a mandatory screening criterion, not an optional factor).

- **Middle Eastern and Asian investors** increasingly seek co-investment rights alongside the main fund — the ability to invest directly alongside the GP at the asset level without paying management fees on that co-investment tranche.

**3. Fee sensitivity and alignment concerns:**

Institutional investors universally scrutinise the "2 and 20" fee model (2% management fee + 20% Promote). Larger LPs negotiate fee discounts and lower hurdle rates in exchange for early commitments ("anchor LP" incentives). The shift from deal-by-deal to whole-fund Promote calculation has been driven primarily by large institutional LPs demanding better downside protection.

---

**三大维度：不同机构投资者的关注点不同**

| 投资者类型 | 负债特征 | 偏好策略 | 关键关注点 |
|---------|---------|---------|----------|
| 养老金 Pension Fund | 长期、通胀挂钩负债 | 核心/核心增益 Core | 长租约、稳定分红、流动性 |
| 大学捐赠基金 Endowment | 跨代际权益 | 增值型/机会型 Value-Add | 更高 IRR，容忍流动性差 |

## Q2a — IO loan monthly payments and balloon payment [5 pts]

*(Lecture 6)*

### Standard Answer

**Interest Only (IO) loan:**
- Loan amount: £5,000,000
- Annual interest rate: 5%
- Monthly interest rate: 5% / 12 = 0.41667%

**Monthly payment:**
$$\text{Monthly payment} = 0.41667\% \times £5{,}000{,}000 = £20{,}833.33$$

**Balloon payment at end of Year 5:**
Since the loan is interest-only, no principal is repaid during the term. The entire principal is due at maturity:
$$\text{Balloon payment} = £5{,}000{,}000$$

Total final period cash outflow = £20,833.33 (interest) + £5,000,000 (principal) = **£5,020,833.33**

---

### 中文详解

**IO 贷款（Interest Only）的本质：** 每期只还利息，本金全部在最后一期（Balloon）归还。

- 月利率 = 5% / 12 = 0.4167%
- 月还款 = 0.4167% × 5,000,000 = **£20,833.33**
- 第60期（Year 5末）：还利息 £20,833.33 + 归还本金 £5,000,000

**计算器操作**：N=1, I/Y=5/12, PV=5,000,000, FV=0 → PMT = 20,833.33

## Q2b — CPM monthly payments and outstanding balance at Year 5 [10 pts]

*(Lecture 6)*

### Standard Answer

**Constant Payment Mortgage (CPM):**
- Loan: £5,000,000
- Amortisation period: 10 years = **120 monthly periods**
- Monthly interest rate: 5% / 12 = 0.41667%

**Step 1: Calculate monthly payment using TVM:**

$$N = 120, \quad I/Y = 5/12, \quad PV = 5{,}000{,}000, \quad FV = 0$$
$$\text{CPT PMT} = £53{,}032.76$$

**Step 2: Outstanding balance after Year 5 (60 periods):**

After 60 payments, the remaining balance is the PV of the remaining 60 payments discounted at the same monthly rate:

$$N = 60, \quad I/Y = 5/12, \quad PMT = 53{,}032.76, \quad FV = 0$$
$$\text{CPT PV} = £2{,}810{,}243.29$$

---

### 中文详解

**CPM（Constant Payment Mortgage 等额还款）的逻辑：** 每月还款额固定，但其中利息占比随时间递减、本金占比递增（amortisation）。

**两步计算：**

1. **月还款额**：TVM 计算器 → N=120, I/Y=0.4167, PV=5,000,000, FV=0 → PMT = **£53,032.76**

2. **Year 5末余额**：重新设 N=60（剩余期数），PMT=53,032.76，计算 PV = **£2,810,243.29**

**关键概念**：CPM 10年摊还但5年后提前还款 → 还有超过一半本金未还（2,810,243 / 5,000,000 ≈ 56%），因为早期还款中大部分是利息。

## Q2c — Effective cost of borrowing for IO and CPM with 0.5% discount point; compare [15 pts]

*(Lecture 6–7)*

### Standard Answer

**Key charges:**
- Discount point: 0.5% × £5,000,000 = **£25,000** (applies to both loans — reduces net proceeds at origination)
- Pre-payment penalty: 0.5% of outstanding balance at Year 5 (applies only to CPM, since the IO loan is already structured for a 5-year term)

**IO Loan — Effective Annual Cost (IRR):**

From the bank's perspective (cash outflows and inflows):
- Net proceeds lent to borrower: £5,000,000 − £25,000 = **£4,975,000** → CF0 = −£4,975,000
- Monthly interest payments: CF1–CF59 = £20,833.33
- Final payment: £20,833.33 + £5,000,000 = £5,020,833.33

Solving for monthly IRR and annualising:

$$\text{Annual IRR (IO)} = \boxed{5.1135\%}$$

**CPM Loan — Effective Annual Cost (IRR):**

- Pre-payment penalty: 0.5% × £2,810,243.29 = **£14,051.22**
- Outstanding balance + penalty: £2,810,243.29 + £14,051.22 = **£2,824,294.51**
- CF0 = −£4,975,000
- CF1–CF59 = £53,032.76
- CF60 = £53,032.76 + £2,824,294.51 = £2,877,327.27

$$\text{Annual IRR (CPM)} = \boxed{5.203\%}$$

**Comparison and discussion:**

The CPM is more expensive to the borrower (5.203% vs 5.114%). Two forces drive this:
1. The **pre-payment penalty** (which applies only to the CPM) adds a cost to early repayment.
2. More importantly, the discount point is applied to the same initial loan amount, but for the CPM the borrower has less outstanding principal over time due to amortisation — so the economic drag of the discount point is spread over a shrinking balance, making the effective cost slightly higher.

The IO loan, having no amortisation and no pre-payment penalty, has a cleaner, lower effective cost when the investor exits at Year 5.

---

### 中文详解

**折扣点 Discount Point 的本质：** 贷款时银行先扣一部分费用（"origination fee"），所以借款人实际到手资金 < 贷款额，但后续还款基于全额贷款计算 → 实际融资成本 > 名义利率。

**IO 有效成本计算流程：**
- CF0 = -(5,000,000 - 25,000) = -4,975,000（扣去折扣点）
- CF1-59 = 20,833.33
- CF60 = 20,833.33 + 5,000,000 = 5,020,833.33
- 用计算器 CF Worksheet + IRR 功能 → 月 IRR × 12 = **5.1135%**

**CPM 有效成本：IO的基础上加一个提前还款罚金（Prepayment Penalty）：**
- CF60 变为：53,032.76 + 2,810,243.29 × 1.005 = 2,877,327.27
- IRR = **5.203%**

**结论**：CPM 实际成本更高，主要因为提前还款罚金（IO贷款已经是5年期，不算"提前"还款）。

**关键词**：Discount Point 折扣点 / Prepayment Penalty 提前还款罚金 / IRR 内部收益率 / Effective Cost 实际融资成本

## Q2d — Rationale for origination charges; banks' preference for CPM; borrowers' preference for IO [5 pts]

*(Lecture 6–7)*

### Standard Answer

**Why banks impose origination charges (discount points):**

Discount points compensate the bank for the **fixed costs of loan origination** — credit underwriting, legal fees, property valuation, loan documentation. These costs are incurred at inception regardless of loan size and must be recovered. Additionally, origination fees are earned up-front and are therefore not subject to prepayment risk.

**What banks like in a CPM compared to an IO:**

1. **Amortisation reduces credit risk** — as the borrower progressively repays principal, the outstanding balance falls, reducing the bank's exposure over time. If the borrower defaults in Year 4, the bank recovers more than if the full principal were still outstanding.

2. **Lower LTV over time** — regular principal repayment improves the loan-to-value ratio, providing a larger equity buffer as collateral.

3. **Predictable income** — constant monthly payments are easy to model and service.

**Why borrowers prefer IO loans:**

1. **Lower monthly cash outflows** — IO payments (£20,833) are far lower than CPM payments (£53,033), improving short-term cash flow.

2. **Capital efficiency** — the borrower retains cash that would otherwise be used for principal repayment, and can redeploy it at a higher return if the investment performs well.

3. **Avoids premature de-leveraging** — if the property appreciates, the borrower benefits from the full leveraged return. Early amortisation reduces the outstanding loan, which reduces the leverage multiplier effect.

---

### 中文详解

**银行为什么喜欢 CPM？**
- 摊还机制降低信用风险：Year 4 违约时，银行收回的钱更多
- LTV（贷款价值比）随时间下降 → 抵押担保更充足

**借款人为什么喜欢 IO？**
- 月还款额低：IO £20,833 vs CPM £53,033 → 节省现金流用于再投资
- 保持高杠杆：IO 贷款本金不减少 → 杠杆乘数效应完全发挥到退出
- 关键：如果物业升值，高杠杆 = 高股权回报（后面 Q2f 会量化这一点）

**关键词**：Origination Fee 开设费 / Amortisation 摊还 / LTV 贷款价值比 / De-leveraging 去杠杆 / Capital Efficiency 资本效率

## Q2f — Leveraged annual IRRs with IO and CPM; compare and discuss [25 pts]

*(Lecture 3)*

### Standard Answer

**IO Leveraged IRR:**

Equity invested:
$$\text{CF0} = -(£10{,}000{,}000 - £4{,}975{,}000) = -£5{,}025{,}000$$

(Purchase price minus net loan proceeds after discount point)

Monthly net cash flows (equity perspective):
- CF1–CF59: £62,500 − £20,833.33 = **£41,666.67** (NOI minus IO interest)
- CF60: (£12,500,000 − £5,000,000) + (£62,500 − £20,833.33) = **£7,541,666.67**

(Sale proceeds minus loan repayment, plus final month net NOI)

$$\text{Annual Leveraged IRR (IO)} = \boxed{16.3759\%}$$

---

**CPM Leveraged IRR:**

Equity invested: CF0 = −£5,025,000 (same — same discount point)

Monthly net cash flows:
- CF1–CF59: £62,500 − £53,032.76 = **£9,467.24** (NOI minus CPM payment)
- CF60: (£12,500,000 − £2,824,294.51) + (£62,500 − £53,032.76) = **£9,685,172.73**

(Sale proceeds minus outstanding balance plus penalty, plus final month net)

$$\text{Annual Leveraged IRR (CPM)} = \boxed{14.85\%}$$

---

**Comparison and discussion:**

| | Unleveraged | IO Leveraged | CPM Leveraged |
|--|------------|-------------|--------------|
| Annual IRR | 11.25% | **16.38%** | **14.85%** |

Both loans generate **positive leverage**: leveraged IRR > unleveraged IRR > loan cost. This is expected — the cost of debt (5.11%/5.20%) is well below the asset's return (11.25%), so borrowed money earns more than it costs.

**Why is IO more advantageous than CPM?**

The key is not primarily the pre-payment penalty (which is small). The decisive factor is **amortisation**. The CPM forces the borrower to repay principal early, reducing the monthly net cash flows significantly (£9,467 vs £41,667 for IO). Although the CPM results in a lower loan balance at exit (£2.82M vs £5.0M to repay), the early cash flows carry more time-value weight in the IRR calculation. De-leveraging early reduces the compound growth effect. In short: **holding more debt for longer (IO) amplifies returns more effectively than paying it down (CPM)**, as long as the asset return exceeds the borrowing cost.

---

### 中文详解

**杠杆 IRR 的逻辑：**

- 无杠杆 IRR 11.25% > 借款成本（IO 5.11%, CPM 5.20%）
- → 借来的钱比它的成本"赚得更多" → 放大股权回报 → **正向杠杆**

**为什么 IO 的杠杆 IRR（16.38%）> CPM（14.85%）？**

- CPM 摊还让早期现金流大幅减少（£9,467 vs £41,667/月）
- 虽然 CPM 退出时还的本金少（£2.82M vs £5M），但早期低现金流的 IRR 损失 > 晚期少还本金的 IRR 收益
- 结论：**越晚还本金（IO），越能放大股权 IRR**

**计算器操作要点：**
- IO: CF0=-5,025,000; C01=41,666.67, F01=59; C02=7,541,666.67, F02=1 → IRR/Y × 12
- CPM: CF0=-5,025,000; C01=9,467.24, F01=59; C02=9,685,172.73, F02=1 → IRR/Y × 12

**关键词**：Positive Leverage 正向杠杆 / Amortisation 摊还 / De-leveraging 去杠杆 / Equity IRR 股权内部收益率

## Q2g — Development loan: how much borrowed and interest payments? [10 pts]

*(Lecture 4)*

### Standard Answer

**Remodelling parameters:**
- Building is empty for 6 months (zero NOI during this period)
- Total remodelling cost: 5% × £10,000,000 = **£500,000**
- Costs disbursed equally over 6 months: £500,000 / 6 = **£83,333.33/month**
- The bank provides a short-term IO development loan to cover cumulative remodelling costs

**How much is borrowed:**

The bank provides funds to cover the full cumulative remodelling costs as they are incurred. Since the monthly draw-down is used to offset the £83,333 cost each month, the borrower needs the bank to advance the full £500,000 progressively. In practice, the bank lends the total **£500,000** (the full remodelling budget), which neutralises the out-of-pocket construction costs.

$$\text{Total Development Loan} = \boxed{£500{,}000}$$

**Interest payments:**

Development loan rate: 7.5% annually = 0.625% per month

$$\text{Monthly interest} = 0.625\% \times £500{,}000 = \boxed{£3{,}125 \text{ per month}}$$

Over 6 months, total interest = £3,125 × 6 = **£18,750**

At month 6: borrower also repays the **£500,000 principal** (balloon).

---

### 中文详解

**开发贷款（Development Loan）的逻辑：**

- 翻新总成本 = 5% × 10,000,000 = **£500,000**
- 银行提供 £500,000 的短期 IO 开发贷款，中和施工成本
- 借款人实际没有额外的现金支出用于翻新（银行的钱抵消了施工费）
- 借款人只需支付开发贷款利息 = 7.5%/12 × 500,000 = **£3,125/月**

## Q2h — Leveraged IRR with remodelling + IO loan; compare with development-loan-only [20 pts]

*(Lecture 3–4)*

### Standard Answer

**Scenario 1: Main IO loan + Development loan (full leveraged scenario)**

Cash flow structure:

- **CF0**: −£5,025,000 (equity = £10M purchase − £4,975M net IO loan proceeds)
  *(Development loan funds cover construction costs, not equity)*

- **CF1–CF5** (months 1–5, building empty, works ongoing):
  £0 − £20,833.33 (IO interest) − £3,125 (dev loan interest) = **−£23,958.33**

- **CF6** (month 6, final construction + dev loan repayment):
  £0 − £20,833.33 − £3,125 − £500,000 (dev loan repayment) = **−£523,958.33**

- **CF7–CF59** (months 7–59, 53 periods, building operational):
  £62,500 − £20,833.33 = **£41,666.67**

- **CF60** (final month + sale):
  (£12,500,000 − £5,000,000) + (£62,500 − £20,833.33) = **£7,541,666.67**

$$\text{Annual Leveraged IRR (IO + Dev Loan)} = \boxed{12.5204\%}$$

---

**Scenario 2: Development loan ONLY (no main IO loan)**

Cash flow structure:

- **CF0**: −£10,000,000 (full equity purchase)

- **CF1–CF5**: £0 − £3,125 (dev loan interest only) = **−£3,125**

- **CF6**: £0 − £3,125 − £500,000 (dev loan repayment) = **−£503,125**

- **CF7–CF59**: £62,500 (full NOI, no main loan charges)

- **CF60**: £12,500,000 + £62,500 = **£12,562,500**

$$\text{Annual IRR (Dev Loan Only)} = \boxed{9.2889\%}$$

---

**Comparison and discussion:**

| Scenario | Annual IRR |
|---------|-----------|
| Unleveraged (Q2e) | 11.25% |
| IO loan only (Q2f) | 16.38% |
| IO + Development loan | **12.52%** |
| Development loan only | **9.29%** |

**Key findings:**

1. **Adding remodelling costs reduces returns**: comparing IO+Dev (12.52%) to IO-only (16.38%) shows the 6-month void and interest costs significantly dilute IRR — the zero-NOI period and construction interest destroy early cash flow.

2. **Development loan only (9.29%) is below the unleveraged IRR (11.25%)**: this is counterintuitive at first glance, but it is correct. The development loan charges 7.5% interest during a period when the asset generates no income — so the borrower is paying for money that produces no return during those months. This negative-leverage effect (loan cost > return during the void period) drags the IRR below the unleveraged return. Unlike the main IO loan (which provides positive leverage by financing an income-producing asset), the development loan is financing a void — so it creates a drag rather than amplification.

---

### 中文详解

**两种场景对比（考试核心）：**

**场景1（IO主贷款 + 开发贷款）：**
- 前6个月：无 NOI，但需付 IO 利息 + 开发贷款利息 → 现金流为负
- CF0 依然是 -5,025,000（开发贷款用于抵消施工成本，不降低首付）
- 结果 IRR = 12.52%（比 IO-only 16.38% 低，因为空置期拖累了早期现金流）

**场景2（仅开发贷款）：**
- 前6个月：零 NOI + 只付开发贷款利息 £3,125/月
- 第6个月：还清 £500,000 开发贷款本金
- 结果 IRR = 9.29% < 无杠杆 IRR（11.25%）

**为什么开发贷款单独用反而拖低 IRR？**
- 开发贷款利率 7.5% > 无杠杆 IRR 11.25%吗？不对！
- 关键是：开发期间 **资产不产生收益**（NOI=0），但贷款利息仍在计数
- 等效于：在亏损期间使用杠杆 → **负向杠杆（Negative Leverage）**
- 对比主贷款：资产稳定产生 NOI > 贷款成本 → 正向杠杆

**关键词**：Void Period 空置期 / Negative Leverage 负向杠杆 / Development Finance 开发融资 / IRR drag IRR 拖拽效应
